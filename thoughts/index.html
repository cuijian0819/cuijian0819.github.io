Starting with the problem

14 Sept

Some thoughts on computer science education as building software becomes easier.

Writing my teaching statement has made me ask what a good computer science education should offer students today. AI tools have made it easier to build software and explore unfamiliar ideas. That makes me want to give more attention to a question that can get lost in the work of implementation: what is worth building, and why?

In computer security, that question leads us back to the world. We care about how systems are actually used, what adversaries can do, and who might be harmed. A technically interesting problem can matter very little outside the assumptions we have made about it. Learning to question those assumptions seems as important as learning to implement a solution.

I would like more of my teaching to start there. What have students noticed? What do they care about? What seems wrong, confusing, or worth investigating? A student might become curious about authorization in an AI agent because a friend encountered something unexpected. That curiosity can give them a reason to learn how the system works and, eventually, enough understanding to investigate whether something is wrong.

Foundations still matter, and how much to teach upfront depends on the course. Students need an initial map: what systems exist, what they do, and roughly how they work. They also deserve to hear my judgment about what matters and why, with room to question it. Knowing that OAuth exists and what problem it addresses gives a student somewhere to begin. A concrete question can then motivate a deeper understanding of the protocol. AI can help with that learning, though students still need to learn how to check an explanation against the system itself.

The part I keep returning to is how much this depends on other people. Students arrive with different experiences, and those experiences shape what they notice. Someone may recognize a problem that would never have occurred to me. I want the classroom to be a place where they can explain what they see, hear other perspectives, and revise their ideas. Caring about a problem is a starting point; discussion and investigation help us understand whether it is the problem we thought it was.

That also changes what I want to assess. A proposal, a paper, or a piece of software can show how a student understands a problem, what evidence supports their judgment, and how they respond to criticism. I do not yet know how to grade this well. Exams may still serve a purpose, but in a course centered on real problems, I want students’ developing judgment to count.

Ultimately, the reason I care about this is human. People experience the world differently and have different reasons to want something changed. I want education to help students take those reasons seriously, communicate them clearly, and develop the understanding to act on them. Building becomes meaningful when we understand whom it serves and why it matters.

Guarantees, not guardrails

1 Aug

In our work on multi-tool agents, the attack begins with a tool provider advertising a capability that sounds genuinely useful, more precise data or a better result, so the model comes to depend on it, and from there the tool can hijack the control flow to harvest or pollute what passes through. What makes this hard is that the model is arguably behaving correctly: if a tool really does offer more precise information, calling it is the right decision. That is why I have stopped treating prompt injection as a category with a clean boundary. There is no reliable distinction between an injected instruction and legitimate information arriving from an external source, and alignment can raise the odds that a model notices, but it cannot guarantee it. Worse, we often want external content to influence behaviour, since ignoring it defeats the point of giving an agent tools at all. So the useful question is not how to detect the bad instruction, because in the general case that detection is not decidable.

The analogy I keep returning to is the phishing email with a malicious attachment. You should absolutely teach people not to open it, and that work is worth doing, but the reason modern systems survive is that the machine assumes the attachment will sometimes be opened and then constrains what it can reach: no sensitive files, no outbound connections. Agents need the same shift, from filtering the input to providing guarantees on the action space, which is what usage control for agentic systems means to me: a policy that holds regardless of what the model was persuaded to believe. That reframing suggests two concrete moves. Stop handing web agents raw click and type primitives, and instead expose higher-level actions defined by the site’s own developer, so that “search for a flight” becomes one auditable operation a user can see and authorise rather than a hundred opaque clicks. And for coding agents, which write and execute a great deal of Python, have the code carry a proof of the security properties we care about, checked before it is translated down to the code that actually runs. In both cases the model stays untrusted, and the guarantee lives somewhere it cannot argue with.
